How the program began

Measured at your health system, against your own baseline

The founding results show what the model can do. At your hospital, the program is measured the same careful way, with your clinical, analytics and finance leaders.

One baseline

Computed from your records before go-live and agreed by your clinical, analytics and finance leaders.

A fair comparison

Patients enroll after a period of high use, so a simple before-and-after can overstate the effect. Each engagement agrees a comparison that accounts for it.

Fidelity alongside outcomes

Huddles held, commitments closed on time and patients placed on the unit, reported monthly with results.

Value your finance team validates

Capacity and cost benefits counted separately and never twice, with your finance team.

The patients behind the numbers

Every hospital has patients like these three: often called hard to place, and admitted again and again. Each story shows one of the outcomes above in a single patient's care.

Recurrent hospitalization

The admission that didn't happen

A man in his late 50s admitted with heart failure and respiratory failure, whose behavioral needs were making discharge hard to plan.

What the team did: Paired careful heart failure care with a structured behavioral plan, and coordinated early with his group home.

BeforeRepeated recent admissions, unstable housing
NowBack at his group home, with follow-up in place

Heart failure (HFpEF)Intellectual disabilityChronic suicidal ideationCocaine use disorderHypertension, diabetes, asthma
1What made it hard

Two kinds of instability at once

He needed careful diuresis for fluid overload while also living with agitation and long-standing self-harm risk. Together these lengthened his stay and complicated discharge, though he did not meet criteria for psychiatric admission.

2What the team did

Medical, behavioral and social care, together

  • A structured behavioral plan built on positive reinforcement
  • Early coordination with his group home
  • Tailored diuresis, oxygen weaning and guideline-directed therapy
  • Psychiatric medication optimized, with close monitoring
3The outcome

Stable, and home

He was weaned to room air at rest and returned to his group home, with ongoing case management and social work support from the program.

  • No psychiatric admission needed
  • Medically stabilized
  • Cardiology and sleep medicine follow-up arranged

1What made it hard

Medically ready was not the same as ready to leave

Frailty, fluctuating delirium, behavioral instability and few post-acute options kept her in hospital. Medical stabilization and discharge planning were moving on separate tracks, so her stay grew longer and admissions recurred.

2What the team did

Medical, behavioral and placement work in parallel

  • Tailored neurobehavioral management when standard approaches did not work

  • Close work across medicine, nursing, pharmacy and case management

1What made it hard

Each admission started over

Readmissions tied to missed medications, psychiatric instability and difficulty sticking with treatment kept getting in the way of discharge planning and safe transitions. She was admitted with a traumatic injury and serious complications that followed.

2What the team did

One plan across every specialty

  • Coordinated medicine, orthopedics, infectious disease and psychiatry
  • Tailored neurobehavioral management
  • Started skilled nursing facility planning early
  • Behavioral strategies that supported an opioid taper and adherence

Over time, the team built enough trust with her to make a safe transition possible.

3The outcome

A safe transition, then lasting stability

She completed IV antibiotics, stabilized medically and behaviorally, and moved to a skilled nursing facility with a structured taper plan, with no ICU transfer or readmission during the episode.

  • Stabilized in the ambulatory setting
  • Living in a group home
  • No longer needs a conservator
What these cases share
Patients often seen as hard to place can reach safe, stable discharges when one team owns the plan.

For the hospital, that means fewer repeat admissions, more reliable discharges and beds freed for the patients waiting for them.
